# Sampling Tutorial 4: Variable-length Wavetable
In this tutorial, we look at the [wave~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/wave~/ "wave~") object, which allows you to access [buffer~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/buffer~/ "buffer~") data as a wavetable for an oscillator. This allows you to load samples and use them as waveforms which are accessed based on playback frequency. You can use these wavetables to build sample-based oscillators for more complex synthesis patches.
## Use any part of a buffer~ as a wavetable: wave~
As was shown in an earlier tutorial, the [cycle~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/cycle~/ "cycle~") object can read samples of a [buffer~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/buffer~/ "buffer~") as a wavetable through which it reads repeatedly to play a periodically repeating tone. The [wave~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/wave~/ "wave~") object is an extension of that idea; it allows you to use _any_ section of a [buffer~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/buffer~/ "buffer~") as a wavetable with detailed control of the endpoints.
The start and end points within the [buffer~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/buffer~/ "buffer~") are determined by the number or signal received in the middle and right inlets of the [wave~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/wave~/ "wave~") object. As a signal in the [wave~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/wave~/ "wave~") object's left inlet goes from `0.` to `1.`, [wave~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/wave~/ "wave~") sends out the contents of the [buffer~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/buffer~/ "buffer~") from the specified start point to the end point. The [phasor~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/phasor~/ "phasor~") object, ramping repeatedly from 0 to 1, is the obvious choice as an input signal for the left inlet of [wave~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/wave~/ "wave~").
In a standard implementation of wavetable synthesis, the wavetable (16K samples in the case of [cycle~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/cycle~/ "cycle~"), or a section of any length in the case of [wave~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/wave~/ "wave~")) would be one single cycle of a waveform, and the frequency of the [cycle~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/cycle~/ "cycle~") object (or the [phasor~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/phasor~/ "phasor~") driving the [wave~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/wave~/ "wave~")) would determine the fundamental frequency of the tone. In the case of [wave~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/wave~/ "wave~"), however, the wavetable could contain virtually anything (an entire spoken sentence, for example).
[wave~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/wave~/ "wave~") yields rather unpredictable results compared to some of the more traditional sound generation ideas presented so far, but with some experimentation you can find a great variety of timbres using [wave~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/wave~/ "wave~"). In this tutorial patch, you will see some ways of reading the contents of a [buffer~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/buffer~/ "buffer~") with [wave~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/wave~/ "wave~").
## Synthesis with a segment of sampled sound
The tutorial patch is designed to let you try three different ways of driving [wave~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/wave~/ "wave~") (labeled `1`, `2`, and `3`) with two different audio samples (labeled `A` and `B`): with a repeating ramp signal ([phasor~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/phasor~/ "phasor~")), a sinusoid ([cycle~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/cycle~/ "cycle~")), or a single ramp ([line~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/line~/ "line~")). The bottom part of the patcher is devoted to the basic implementation of [wave~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/wave~/ "wave~"), and the upper part of the window contains the three methods of reading through the wavetable. First, let's look at the bottom half of the window.
  * Turn audio on. Set the amplitude to a comfortable level. Click the `message` box labeled `A`. This will instruct the [wave~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/wave~/ "wave~") object to use the [buffer~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/buffer~/ "buffer~") named `words` and will set the length of the wavetable to the entire length of the sample. Now we are ready to read through the wavetable.
  * Choose ‘Read forward’ from the pop-up [umenu](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/umenu/ "umenu") in the middle of the window. This will open the first signal inlet of the [selector~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/selector~/ "selector~"), allowing [wave~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/wave~/ "wave~") to be controlled by the [phasor~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/phasor~/ "phasor~") object in the circuit in the upper-left (labeled `1`).
  * Set the [number](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/number/ "number") box marked ‘Range’ to `1`. This sets the amplitude of the [phasor~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/phasor~/ "phasor~"), so it effectively determines what fraction of the wavetable will be used. Set the [number](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/number/ "number") box marked ‘Frequency’ to `2`. The [phasor~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/phasor~/ "phasor~") now goes from 0 to 1 two times per second, so you should hear [wave~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/wave~/ "wave~") reading through the [buffer~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/buffer~/ "buffer~") every half second.
  * Try a few different sub-audio frequency values for the [phasor~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/phasor~/ "phasor~"), to read through the [buffer~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/buffer~/ "buffer~") at different speeds. You can change the portion of the [buffer~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/buffer~/ "buffer~") being read, either by changing the ‘Range’ value, or by changing the start and end times of the [wave~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/wave~/ "wave~") at the bottom of the patcher. Try audio frequencies for the [phasor~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/phasor~/ "phasor~") as well.


Notice that the rate of the [phasor~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/phasor~/ "phasor~") often has no obvious relationship to the perceived pitch, because the contents of the wavetable do not represent a single cycle of a waveform. Furthermore, such rapid repetition of an arbitrarily selected segment of a complex sample has a very high likelihood of producing frequencies well in excess of the Nyquist rate, which will be folded back into the audible range in unpredictable ways.
  * Click on the [message](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/message/ "message") box labeled `B` to refer [wave~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/wave~/ "wave~") to the [buffer~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/buffer~/ "buffer~") object named `chords`.


This changes the contents of the wavetable (because [wave~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/wave~/ "wave~") now accesses a different [buffer~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/buffer~/ "buffer~")), and sets the value of the ‘End time’ [number](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/number/ "number") box equal to the length of the file _sacre.aiff_. Notice an additional little programming trick - shown in the example below - employed to prevent the user from entering inappropriate start and end times for [wave~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/wave~/ "wave~").
![](https://docs.cycling74.com/images/e31d99fdf06cc6d6b77711855c9e304d_471.webp) Each time the start or end time is changed, it revises the limits of the other number box
  * With this new [buffer~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/buffer~/ "buffer~"), experiment further by reading different length segments of the [buffer~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/buffer~/ "buffer~") at various rates.


## Using wave~ as a transfer function
Take a look at the section of patcher logic labeled `2`. The [buffer~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/buffer~/ "buffer~") object can also be used as a lookup table that can be accessed in different ways by other objects. In the next tutorial we'll look at this in a more traditional manner, but with the [wave~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/wave~/ "wave~") object you can generate interesting timbres by feeding a simple waveform into the object as an index value. To use [wave~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/wave~/ "wave~") in this way, we must scale and offset the incoming [cycle~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/cycle~/ "cycle~") so that it sends signal values from 0 to 1.
  * Set the start and end times of [wave~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/wave~/ "wave~") close together, so that only a few milliseconds of sound are being used for the wavetable. Choose ‘Read back and forth’ from the pop-up [umenu](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/umenu/ "umenu") in the middle of the window. This opens the second signal inlet of the [selector~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/selector~/ "selector~"), allowing [wave~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/wave~/ "wave~") to be controlled by the [cycle~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/cycle~/ "cycle~") object.
  * Set the ‘Range’ [number](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/number/ "number") box to a very small value such as `0.01` at first, to limit the [cycle~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/cycle~/ "cycle~") object's amplitude. This way, [cycle~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/cycle~/ "cycle~") will use a very small segment of the wavetable as the transfer function. Set the frequency of [cycle~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/cycle~/ "cycle~") to `220` Hz. You will probably hear a rich tone with a fundamental frequency of 220 Hz. Drag on the ‘Range’[number box](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/number/)to change the amplitude of the cosine wave; the timbre will change accordingly. You can also experiment with different wavetable lengths by changing the start and end times of [wave~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/wave~/ "wave~"). Sub-audio frequencies for the [cycle~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/cycle~/ "cycle~") object will produce unusual vibrato-like effects as it scans back and forth through the wavetable.


## Play the segment as a note
Look at the third section of patcher logic at the top, labeled `3`. Because [wave~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/wave~/ "wave~") accepts any signal input in the rage 0 to 1, you can read through the wavetable just once by sending [wave~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/wave~/ "wave~") a ramp signal from 0 to 1 (or backward, from 1 to 0). Other objects such as [play~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/play~/ "play~") and [groove~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/groove~/ "groove~") are better suited for this purpose, but it is nevertheless possible with [wave~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/wave~/ "wave~").
  * Choose ‘Read once’ from the pop-up [umenu](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/umenu/ "umenu") in the middle of the window. This opens the third signal inlet of the [selector~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/selector~/ "selector~"), allowing [wave~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/wave~/ "wave~") to be controlled by the [line~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/line~/ "line~") object. Set start and end times for your wavetable, set the ‘Duration’ [number](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/number/ "number") box to `1000`, and click on the [button](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/button/ "button") to traverse the wavetable in one second. Experiment with both [buffer~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/buffer~/ "buffer~") objects, using various wavetable lengths and durations.


## Changing the wavetable dynamically
The [cycle~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/cycle~/ "cycle~") object in the right part of the Patcher window is used to add a sinusoidal position change to the wavetable. As the cosine wave rises and falls, the start and end times of the wavetable increase and decrease. As a result, the wavetable is constantly shifting its position in the [buffer~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/buffer~/ "buffer~"), in a sinusoidally varying manner. Sonically this produces a unique sort of vibrato, not of fundamental frequency but of timbre. The wavetable length and the rate at which it is being read stay the same, but the wavetable's contents are continually changing.
  * Set the ‘Shift amount’ to `0.35`, and set the ‘Shift rate’ to `6`. Set the start time of the wavetable to `102` and the end time to `109`. Click on the [message](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/message/ "message") box to refer [wave~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/wave~/ "wave~") to the [buffer~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/buffer~/ "buffer~")`chords` object. Choose ‘Read forward’ from the pop-up [umenu](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/umenu/ "umenu"). Set the frequency of the [phasor~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/phasor~/ "phasor~") to an audio rate such as `110`, and set its range to `1`. You should hear a vibrato-like timbre change at the rate of 6 Hz. Experiment with varying the shift rate and the shift amount. When you are done, click on the [toggle](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/toggle/ "toggle") to turn audio off.


## Summary
Any segment of the contents of a [buffer~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/buffer~/ "buffer~") can be used as a wavetable for the [wave~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/wave~/ "wave~") object. You can read through the wavetable by sending a signal to [wave~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/wave~/ "wave~") that goes from 0 to 1. So, by connecting the output of a [phasor~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/phasor~/ "phasor~") object to the input of [wave~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/wave~/ "wave~"), you can read through the wavetable repeatedly at a sub-audio or audio rate. You can also scale and offset the output of a [cycle~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/cycle~/ "cycle~") object so that it is in the range 0 to 1, and use that as input to [wave~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/wave~/ "wave~"). This treats the wavetable as a transfer function, and results in waveshaping synthesis. The position of the wavetable in the [buffer~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/buffer~/ "buffer~") can be varied dynamically - by adding a sinusoidal offset to the start and end times of [wave~](https://docs.cycling74.com/reference/wave~/ "wave~"), for example - resulting in unique sorts of timbre modulation.
